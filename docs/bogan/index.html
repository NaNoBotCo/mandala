Righto

Mandala means circle

Mandala. Say it: MAN-da-la. Old, old word from India. Means circle. Easy.

Monks make 'em out of coloured sand, grain by grain. Takes 'em flamin' ages. Then they sweep the whole lot up. Strewth.

Holy bit in the middle, rings going out, and out, and out.

Here's the thing: the old kings over in Asia ran their whole country that same shape. Fair dinkum.

Have a go. Draw with your finger. It copies itself all the way round.

Now sweep it up. That's what the monks do. Nothing lasts, mate. Kings neither.

Oldest trick in the book

Your neighbour's your enemy

Two thousand-odd years ago a clever bloke in India called Kautilya wrote a book on how to be king.

Drew it as rings. You in the middle. Bloke next door? He's got his eye on your patch. That's your enemy.

Bloke past him? He's got the same problem with your neighbour. So he's your mate.

Enemy, mate, enemy, mate, all the way out. Like the footy, only with elephants.

Tap any kingdom. Now it's the middle, and everyone changes colour.

Tap any kingdom to make it the middle.

Kautilya called it the circle of kings. We'd say: the enemy of my enemy is my mate. Bloody oath.

No fences

Light, not lines

Back then there were no lines on the map. Hardly any maps, deadset.

A kingdom was like the light at a servo on a long highway at night. Bright out the front. Dimmer in the car park. Out on the road, only roos.

Drive far enough and the next servo's light is brighter. Now you're his customer.

Some little towns sat right in between two lights and paid both kings. Old Thai folk called that a town under two skies.

And the kings fought over people, not dirt. Land everywhere, blokes scarce. Whoever had the most people round him was the king.

Drag a king round. Slide to make him shine. Tap a village to see who it pays.

Same shape, every size

Kings inside kings

Every big king had little kings round him. Every little king had littler kings. Right down to the head bloke in your village.

Like the pub: publican, bar manager, head barmaid, the glassie. Each one answers up and bosses down.

Up in the North of Thailand a town was a mueang (say MUE-ang), a village was a baan, and the boss was the chao. Means lord.

Tap a little one to go inside it.

Tribute

Gifts go up, help comes down

Little king sends the big king pressies. Rice. Elephants. Blokes to fight. That's called tribute.

It's shouting a round, really. You shout the big bloke, and you're in.

Down south, the Malay sultans sent Siam a little tree made of gold and one of silver. Bunga mas, gold flowers. Bit fancier than a slab. Chiang Mai sent Bangkok gold and silver trees too, every three years.

Big king sends back a fancy title, a fancy umbrella, and the word: muck with him, you muck with me.

Didn't mean he owned your land. Meant you were his crew. Big difference.

Your turn. You're head bloke of a little town between two big kings. Ten baskets a year. Split 'em up.

The catch

When the king carks it, it all wobbles

All that glow came from the bloke. Not the chair. The bloke.

People reckoned a strong king had heaps of merit, good luck saved up from good deeds. That's what pulled people in.

When he carked it, the little kings got thinking. Is the new fella any good? Some stuck around. Some wandered off to another king.

Like when the old publican retires. Stools get shuffled, the meat tray vanishes, nobody knows whose tab is whose.

1800s

Then the blokes with rulers turned up

The Poms came in one side, the French the other, with pencils and rulers.

They said: where's your fence? Siam said: fence? We've got a middle.

So Siam drew lines, quick smart, so nobody pinched more. A Thai professor, Thongchai Winichakul, wrote a whole book on it: Siam Mapped.

And when Bangkok drew the new boxes, what'd they call 'em? Monthon. Same word as mandala. Stone the crows.

Chiang Mai had lords for six hundred years. Sometimes free, sometimes shouting Burma, sometimes shouting Siam. Bit by bit Bangkok pulled it in. The last ruler of Chiang Mai died in 1939.

You can't put a fence round a smell, mate. They tried anyway. Slide the years and watch.

The good bit

Backwards compatible

Today the North of Thailand's got provinces, districts, a governor, elections. Lines on a map. All official.

But go ask a nan in a village who to see when the ditch floods. She won't say the governor.

She'll say the head man. Then the kamnan, that's the boss of a few villages. Then the big house in town, the family everyone knows. Thai folk call it ban yai. Big house.

That's the old circle, still ticking over under the new map. Like an old game still running on a brand new computer. The computer blokes call that backwards compatible.

Here's the kicker: the head man and kamnan jobs came out of the same 1890s shake-up that ended Chiang Mai's own government. New system, same old rings.

Nobody chucked the old way out. They whacked the new way on top. She'll be right.

Tap a village. Watch both roads to Bangkok light up.

Words

Mandala
Circle. A picture of the world with a holy middle. Also how the old kingdoms were shaped.
Mueang (MUE-ang)
A town and the land round it, in Thai and Lao. Big ones had little ones.
Chao
Lord. The boss of a mueang.
Baan
A village. Also a house.
Tribute
Pressies a little king shouts a big king, to show whose side he's on.
Bunga mas
Gold and silver trees the Malay sultans sent to Siam.
Merit (bun)
Good luck saved up from good deeds. A king needed heaps.
Town under two skies
Mueang song fai fa: a little town that paid two big kings at once. Fa means sky, and lord too. Cheeky.
Kautilya
Old Indian writer. Neighbour's your enemy, neighbour's neighbour's your mate.
Galactic polity
Professor Stanley Tambiah's name for it: a big star, little stars going round.
Geo-body
Thongchai Winichakul's word for a country as a shape on a map.
Ban yai
Big house. A family in a province everyone goes to see.
Kamnan
Boss of a few villages put together, a tambon.
Phu yai baan
Village head man.
Backwards compatible
When the new thing still runs the old thing.
