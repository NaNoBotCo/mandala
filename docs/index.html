First off

Mandala means circle

Mandala. Say it with me: MAN-duh-luh. It's an old, old word from India. It means circle.

Monks make 'em out of colored sand. Takes 'em a coon's age. Then they sweep the whole thing up. Lord have mercy.

There's a holy spot in the middle, and rings going out, and out, and out.

Here's the kicker: old kings in Asia ran their whole country in that exact same shape.

Go on and make you one. Draw with your finger. It copies itself all the way 'round.

Now sweep it away. That's what the monks do. Nothing stays put. Kings neither.

The oldest trick

Your neighbor's your enemy

About two thousand years back, a smart fella in India named Kautilya wrote a book on how to be king.

He drew it as rings. You in the middle. Your next-door neighbor? He's eyeballin' your land. That's your enemy.

The fella past him has got the same problem with that neighbor. So he's your friend.

Enemy, friend, enemy, friend, all the way out. Ain't that the truth.

Tap any kingdom. Now it's the middle, and everybody changes color.

Tap any kingdom to make it the middle.

Folks still say it: the enemy of my enemy is my friend. Kautilya called the whole thing the circle of kings.

No fences

Light, not lines

Back then there weren't no lines on the map. Shoot, there weren't hardly any maps.

A kingdom was like a porch light. Bright up close. Dimmer out in the yard. Out by the road you can't hardly tell.

Walk far enough and the next fella's porch light is brighter. Now you're on his porch.

Some little towns sat smack dab between two lights, and paid both kings. Old-time Thai folks called that a town under two skies.

And kings fought over people, not dirt. Land was everywhere. Folks were scarce. The king with the most folks around him was the king.

Same shape, every size

Kings inside kings

Every big king had little kings around him. Every little king had littler kings. All the way down to the head man in your village.

Like them wooden dolls with a littler doll inside, and a littler one inside that.

Up in the North of Thailand a town was a mueang (say MUE-ang), a village was a baan, and the man on top was the chao. That means lord.

Tap a little one to go inside it.

Tribute

Gifts go up, help comes down

Little king sends the big king presents. Rice. Elephants. Fellas to fight. That's called tribute.

Down south, the Malay sultans sent Siam a little tree made out of gold, and one out of silver. They called it bunga mas, gold flowers. Chiang Mai sent Bangkok gold and silver trees too, every three years.

Big king sends back a fancy title, a fancy umbrella, and the word: anybody messes with you, messes with me.

That didn't mean he owned your land. It meant you were his people. Big difference.

Now you try. You're head man of a little town between two big kings. Ten baskets a year. Split 'em up.

The catch

When the king dies, it all wobbles

All that shine came from the man. Not the chair. The man.

Folks said a strong king had a mess of merit, good luck stored up from good deeds. That's what pulled people in.

When he passed on, the little kings got to wondering. Is the new boy any count? Some stayed. Some drifted off to another king.

So the circles were always swelling up and shrinking down. Like breathing.

1800s

Then the mapmakers showed up

The British came in on one side and the French on the other, with rulers and ink.

They said: where's your line? Siam said: line? We got a middle.

So Siam drew lines, and drew 'em quick, so nobody'd take more. A Thai teacher named Thongchai Winichakul wrote a whole book about it called Siam Mapped.

And when Bangkok drew its new boxes, guess what it called 'em? Monthon. Same old word as mandala. Well I'll be.

Chiang Mai had lords for six hundred years. Sometimes free, sometimes paying Burma, sometimes paying Siam. Bit by bit Bangkok pulled it in close. The last ruler of Chiang Mai passed on in 1939.

The fun part

Backwards compatible

Today the North of Thailand has provinces, districts, a governor, ballots. Lines on a map. All official.

But go ask a grandma in a village who to see when the ditch floods. She won't say the governor.

She'll say the head man. Then the kamnan, that's the boss of a few villages. Then the big house in town, the family everybody knows. Thai folks call that ban yai. Big house.

That's the old circle, still a-running, right under the new map. Like an old game that still plays on a brand new computer. Computer folks call that backwards compatible.

Funny thing: the head man and kamnan jobs came out of the same 1890s shake-up that ended Chiang Mai's own government. The new system grew the same old rings.

Nobody threw the old way out. They laid the new way on top.

Words

Mandala
Circle. A picture of the world with a holy middle. Also how old kingdoms were shaped.
Mueang (MUE-ang)
A town and the land around it, in Thai and Lao. Big ones had little ones.
Chao
Lord. The fella on top of a mueang.
Baan
A village. Also a house.
Tribute
Presents a little king sends a big king, to show he's on his side.
Bunga mas
Gold and silver trees the Malay sultans sent to Siam.
Merit (bun)
Good luck saved up from good deeds. A king needed a whole lot.
Town under two skies
Mueang song fai fa: a little town that paid two big kings at once. Fa means sky, and lord too.
Kautilya
Old Indian writer. Neighbor's your enemy, neighbor's neighbor's your friend.
Galactic polity
A teacher named Stanley Tambiah's name for it: a big star, little stars going round.
Geo-body
Thongchai Winichakul's word for a country as a shape on a map.
Ban yai
Big house. A family in a province everybody goes to see.
Kamnan
Head of a few villages put together, a tambon.
Phu yai baan
Village head man.
Backwards compatible
When the new thing still runs the old thing.
